Recommendation: approve Crowcon for the 3 required gases (CO 0–50 ppm, H₂S 0–25 ppm, infrared LEL). Sensidyne is better in several ways (built-in alarm, H₂S 0–10 ppm, 3-year sensors, 2–4 week lead time), but not by enough to justify about $7,000 more. If the end user wants to detect the odors, add only Sensidyne's PID (≈ $14,600 – $14,700 total).

Our case

The site

  • Administrative office, unclassified area: general-purpose equipment is fine.
  • About 1 km away there is a port where fuel and crude tankers unload; odors reach the office.
  • Goal: a local alarm (visual and audible) when levels get high, with no central panel.

Sensors

  • H₂S in the lowest range available, to get closer to odor levels.
  • CO at breathing height.
  • Infrared LEL as a backup. At 1 km it should read 0%; Sensidyne confirms this.
  • Optional: a PID, the only sensor that sees fuel vapors in ppm.

Glossary

The technical terms on this page and in the quotes, in plain words.

H₂S (hydrogen sulfide)
Toxic gas that smells like rotten eggs. At high levels you stop smelling it because it numbs the sense of smell. Heavier than air, so it collects low.
CO (carbon monoxide)
Toxic gas with no smell or color, produced by engines, boilers and any incomplete combustion. About as heavy as air.
ppm (parts per million)
Unit for small amounts of toxic gas. 10 ppm of H₂S is 10 parts of H₂S per million parts of air.
LEL / % LEL
Lower explosive limit: the lowest concentration at which a gas or vapor can ignite. 20% LEL is one fifth of what it takes for an explosion.
Electrochemical sensor
The usual sensor for H₂S and CO. Accurate and affordable, but it wears out and is replaced every 2–3 years.
Infrared (IR) sensor
Flammable-gas sensor that measures how much infrared light the gas absorbs. H₂S doesn't poison it and it lasts longer (5+ years).
PID (photoionization detector)
Sensor that measures organic vapors in ppm with an ultraviolet lamp. It sees gasoline and diesel at levels an LEL sensor misses, but can't tell compounds apart.
Standalone
A detector that measures, shows the level and triggers its own alarm without a central panel.
Relay
A switch inside the detector that closes at an alarm level. Used to turn on a horn, strobe or fan.
Horn / strobe
The audible alarm (horn, in dB) and the flashing light (strobe or beacon). Sensidyne has them built in; Crowcon needs an external one.
dB (decibels)
How loud a horn is. 40 dB is quieter than a conversation; 95 dB is easy to hear in a workshop.
Alarm setpoints
The levels where the detector warns. The low alarm is a warning; the high alarm means act now. They must fall within the sensor's range.
Classified area / general purpose
A classified area can have flammable vapors and needs certified equipment. An unclassified office can use general-purpose equipment, like both quoted options.
Calibration / bump test
Calibration adjusts the detector with a gas of known concentration (every 6 months). A bump test quickly confirms it responds and the alarm sounds.
Calibration gas and kit
The gas comes in cylinders with an exact mix, one per sensor type. The kit is the hardware: regulator, tubing and adapter. Cylinders are dangerous goods by air.
Spare sensor
The sensor you swap at end of life without replacing the detector. It ages even in storage, so buy it close to the replacement date.
FOB / Incoterms
Terms that set who pays freight and where risk passes. FOB St. Petersburg means the buyer pays freight from Sensidyne's factory.
