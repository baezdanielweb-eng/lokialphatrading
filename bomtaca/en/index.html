Gas detectors for H₂S, CO and fuel vapors

Options for an industrial site where each detector works on its own: no central controller, with a local light (strobe), a horn and, where possible, a screen showing the live gas level. The list covers all-in-one units and one-detector-per-gas setups, in mid and high price ranges.

Our case

The site

  • Administrative office, unclassified area: general-purpose equipment is fine.
  • About 1 km away there is a port where fuel and crude oil tankers unload; odors reach the office.
  • Goal: local alarm (visual and audible) when levels get high, with no central panel.

Chosen sensors

  • H₂S, in the lowest range available, to get closer to odor levels.
  • CO, at breathing height.
  • Infrared LEL, as a safety backstop. At 1 km it will normally read 0; it would only rise with a strong vapor cloud, and that's not a fault.
  • Optional: PID for fuel and crude vapors in ppm/ppb, the most sensitive sensor for these odors.

First: is the area classified?

Where fuel vapors can build up, the area is often classified as hazardous (Class I Div 1/2 in the US, ATEX/IECEx zones elsewhere). In a classified area, the detector, horn and strobe all need the matching explosion-proof rating. The cheaper "general purpose" units below (Macurco, Forensics) are not for classified areas. Your safety officer or the plant's area classification drawing settles this, and the answer decides which half of this page applies.

Our case: unclassified (administrative office). No explosion-proof equipment is needed, so the general-purpose options also apply.

Things that matter for this install

H₂S kills catalytic LEL sensors

The common "catalytic bead" LEL sensor gets poisoned by H₂S and silicones and slowly stops reading. With H₂S on site, choose an infrared (IR) LEL sensor (Crowcon IR, MSA XIR+, Blackline LEL-IR, Radius IR). IR doesn't see hydrogen, but it does see gasoline and most fuel vapors.

Diesel and heavy fuels

Gasoline vapor is easy to detect as % LEL. Diesel, kerosene and jet fuel give off little vapor, so an LEL sensor may read near zero even when the smell is strong. If heavy fuels are the main concern, add a PID sensor (an option on the Radius BZ1 and SensAlarm FLEX).

Mounting height

  • H₂S and gasoline vapor are heavier than air: mount 30–45 cm (12–18 in) above the floor, near pits, drains and low spots.
  • CO is close to air weight: mount in the breathing zone, about 1.2–1.8 m (4–6 ft).
  • That's a big reason to prefer one head per gas over a single box.

Typical alarm levels

  • H₂S: low 10 ppm / high 15–20 ppm
  • CO: low 25 ppm / high 50 ppm
  • Fuel vapor: low 10% LEL / high 20% LEL

Set yours with your safety officer and local rules (OSHA, or your country's equivalent).

Running cost

Plan for a bump test with gas every month or so, a full calibration about every 6 months, and new H₂S/CO sensors every 2–3 years. Calibration gas and a regulator cost a few hundred dollars a year. A cheap unit with expensive or hard-to-find sensors costs more in the long run.

Before you order

  • Area classification (hazardous or not)
  • Power at each point (24 VDC, 120/240 VAC, or battery)
  • Gas ranges: H₂S 0–50 ppm, CO 0–500 ppm, LEL 0–100%
  • Indoor or outdoor (IP rating, temperature)
  • Ask for the horn/strobe in the same quote

Glossary

The technical terms used on this page and in the quotes, in plain words.

H₂S (hydrogen sulfide)
Toxic gas that smells like rotten eggs. At high levels you stop smelling it because it deadens the sense of smell, which is why you need a detector. It is heavier than air and collects in low spots.
CO (carbon monoxide)
Toxic gas with no smell or color, produced by engines, boilers and any incomplete combustion. It weighs about the same as air.
ppm (parts per million)
Unit for measuring toxic gases in small amounts. 10 ppm of H₂S means 10 parts of H₂S per million parts of air.
LEL / % LEL (lower explosive limit)
The lowest concentration of a gas or vapor in air that can ignite. A reading of 20% LEL means there is one fifth of what's needed for an explosion.
Fuel vapors
The gas given off by gasoline, diesel and other liquid fuels. Measured as combustible gas in % LEL, or with a PID sensor for heavy fuels.
Electrochemical sensor
The sensor type normally used for H₂S and CO. Accurate and inexpensive, but it wears out and needs replacing every 2–3 years.
Catalytic (catalytic bead) sensor
Combustible-gas sensor that "burns" a tiny sample to measure it. Cheap and common, but H₂S and silicones poison it and it stops reading correctly without warning.
Infrared (IR) sensor
Combustible-gas sensor that measures how much infrared light the gas absorbs. H₂S doesn't poison it and it lasts longer, but it costs more and can't detect hydrogen.
PID (photoionization detector)
Sensor that measures organic vapors in ppm using an ultraviolet lamp. Works for diesel, kerosene and other heavy fuels an LEL sensor barely sees. The lamp needs periodic cleaning.
Sensor poisoning
Permanent damage to a sensor from exposure to certain chemicals (H₂S, silicones, lead). The sensor stays on but reads lower than reality.
Fixed detector
Unit installed permanently on a wall or post, powered by the plant. It watches an area 24 hours a day.
Personal (portable) monitor
Small detector worn by a worker, with a rechargeable battery. It protects the person wherever they go, but doesn't watch a fixed area.
Transportable area monitor
Battery unit, larger than a personal one, placed at a spot for days or weeks and movable. Examples: Radius BZ1, RigRat.
Sampling (extractive) detector
Unit that pumps air through tubing from the point being watched. The detector itself stays in a safe area. Example: Midas S2.
Head / transmitter
The part of a fixed detector that holds the sensor. A "transmitter" only sends the reading to other equipment; it may have no display or alarm of its own.
Controller / central panel
Panel that receives signals from several detectors and triggers the alarms. This site won't have one, so each detector must alarm on its own.
Standalone
A detector that works alone: it measures, shows the level and triggers its own alarm without a controller.
Relay
A switch inside the detector that trips at an alarm level. Used to turn on a horn, a strobe or a fan.
Horn / strobe
The audible alarm (horn, measured in dB) and the flashing light (strobe or beacon). Some detectors have them built in; others need an external one wired to the relay.
dB (decibels)
How loud a horn is. 95 dB is clearly heard in a workshop; 108 dB carries over a noisy site.
Alarm levels (low / high)
The readings at which the detector warns. Low is a caution; high means evacuate or act immediately.
4–20 mA / Modbus
Standard ways to send a detector's reading to a controller or control system. Not needed for standalone detectors, but useful if a controller is added later.
Classified (hazardous) area
A zone where flammable gas or vapor can be in the air. Only equipment certified not to cause a spark can be installed there.
Class I, Division 1 / Division 2
The US classification for flammable-gas areas. Division 1: gas may be present in normal operation. Division 2: only during a leak or failure. Set by an engineer or local code.
Zone 0 / 1 / 2 (ATEX / IECEx)
The equivalent international classification. Zone 1 roughly matches Division 1, and Zone 2 matches Division 2.
Explosion-proof (XP / Ex d)
A housing built so that if a spark happens inside, the explosion can't escape. Required in classified areas.
General purpose
Equipment with no hazardous-area rating. Only for non-classified zones such as offices, workshops or warehouses without flammable vapors.
IP66 / NEMA 4X
Protection ratings against dust and water. IP66 and NEMA 4X mean it withstands water jets and outdoor use.
Bump test
A quick test that exposes the detector to a small amount of gas to confirm it responds and the alarm sounds. Done periodically.
Calibration
Adjusting the detector with a gas of known concentration so the reading is accurate. Usually every 6 months.
Calibration gas
A cylinder with an exact gas mixture, used for bump tests and calibration. You need one per sensor type.
Calibration kit
The accessories for calibrating: cylinder regulator, tubing, and the adapter or cup that fits over the sensor.
Sensor cartridge
The replacement sensor swapped in when the original reaches end of life, without replacing the whole detector.
Surcharge
An extra charge on top of list price. Honeywell added 16% to many of its products in 2025.
Incoterms
International terms that define who pays shipping, insurance and customs, and where risk passes to the buyer (for example, EXW or DAP).
